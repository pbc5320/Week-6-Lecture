Why it mattersEvery regression, Bayesian model and causal setup later in the course assumes one of these shapes for the noise or the outcome. Choosing the shape is choosing your theory of how the data was made.

Three words that trip people up

PMF
Discrete only. The bar height is the actual probability of that exact count. Bars sum to 1.
PDF
Continuous only. Height is density. Probability is the area under the curve, so one exact value has probability 0. The lecture also calls the discrete plots "PDF".
CDF
The running total: probability of that value or less. Hover the shape view and the shaded part is the CDF.
Check yourself: why does a Poisson have no "n"?

Poisson counts events arriving over time or space, so there is no fixed number of attempts. Binomial fixes n trials and counts successes. If the number of chances is fixed, use binomial; if events just arrive, use Poisson.

Moment lab

A moment is the average of some power of the variable. Different powers capture different features of the shape. Reshape the protest distribution below and watch all four numbers move.

Protests per district: drag the weights

The moment ladder

1st: mean
Where the center is. E[X] = Σ x · P(x).
2nd: variance
How spread out, measured from the mean. E[(X − μ)²]. Standard deviation is its square root, in the original units.
3rd: skewness
Lopsidedness. Positive means a long right tail.
4th: kurtosis
Tail weight. Higher means more rare, extreme values.

Central means measured around the mean. Standardized means also divided by σ, so units cancel.

Why it mattersMuch of political science is comparing first and second moments: the difference in means between two groups, or the variance in two populations. The formulas for each named distribution are just these moments written in terms of the parameters.
Check the lecture's number. With the default weights the variance comes out 1.14. The lecture says 1.04. The shortcut agrees with 1.14: E[X²] − μ² = 3.1 − 1.96.

10:43 – 17:30

Rules that work for any distribution

Three tools that do not care which distribution you have: linearity, the Markov and Chebyshev bounds, and standardizing. They are why so much of the earlier algebra was easy.

Linearity of expectation

Scale and shift a variable, and its mean scales and shifts the same way. Holds for every distribution.

Chebyshev: how much can sit far from the mean?

Knowing only the mean and standard deviation, at most 1/k² of the probability lies more than k standard deviations away. Compare with a normal curve.

Markov: a bound from the average alone

For a variable that cannot go below zero, P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X] / a. Income is the lecture's example.

Standardizing: the z-score

z = (x − μ) / σ counts how many standard deviations a value sits from the mean. It removes units, so any normal variable maps onto one reference curve.

Why it mattersChebyshev gives a guarantee without assuming normality: at least 75% of any distribution lies within 2 SDs. That is useful for long-tailed things like conflict severity, or when all you have is a rough mean and variance.

Method of moments

The bridge from theory to data. A formula says mean = λ. Your data has a sample mean. Set them equal and you have an estimate of λ. A hidden protest rate makes the data below; see how close you get.

Protests per month in one country

Bars: how often each count showed up in your data. Dots: what a Poisson with your estimated λ predicts.

The recipe

  1. Theory. Write the moment as a formula in the parameters. Poisson: E[X] = λ.
  2. Data. Compute the same moment from the sample. Sample mean x̄.
  3. Match. Set them equal and solve. λ̂ = x̄. The hat means "estimate".
Why it mattersQuick, interpretable estimates with no heavy optimization. It also shows exactly how the data informs each parameter.

Same idea, other distributions

Exponential
mean is 1/λ, so λ̂ = 1 / x̄
Binomial (n known)
mean is n·p, so p̂ = x̄ / n
Normal
μ̂ = x̄ and σ̂² is the sample variance
Check yourself: why do estimates wobble at small n?

A small sample is a noisy average. Set n to 5 and draw a few times, then set n to 300. This is the law of large numbers, coming up in chapter 7.

Two variables at once

Social science is about how things move together: mobilization and turnout, repression and protest. A joint distribution describes both at once. The tools that follow are conditional expectation, the law of iterated expectations, covariance and correlation.

Covariance vs correlation

Each dot is a district: ideology score (x) and turnout (y). Blue quadrants add positive co-movement, red ones subtract. Then switch the units of y.

The red line is the average y at each x, an estimate of E[Y | X]. Covariance changes with units. Correlation does not.

Conditional expectation and iterated expectations

E[X | Y] reads "expected X given Y". Say X is protest count and Y is repression: 5 protests expected under low repression, 2 under high. The overall average is the weighted average of these:

E[X] = E[ E[X | Y] ]

Simulation, LLN and CLT

Simulation means having the computer draw many random values from a distribution you choose, then checking your formulas against the results. Two theorems explain why it works: the law of large numbers (where the average is headed) and the central limit theorem (what shape its uncertainty has on the way).

Central limit theorem

Same ground, two theorems

LLN
The sample mean converges to the true mean. The destination.
CLT
The sample mean is approximately normal with variance σ²/n. The road map.
σ/√n
The standard error: how far a sample mean typically lands from the truth.
Why it mattersThe CLT is why the normal shows up everywhere. It underlies standard errors, confidence intervals and hypothesis tests, even when the raw data is skewed.
Small precision. The lecture calls the means of 30 standard normal draws "a standard normal". Their spread is 1/√30 ≈ 0.18, not 1. Drag n and compare the two SD tiles.

The data generating process

A data generating process is your theory of how the data came to exist, written as a formula plus a distribution for the leftover randomness. The lecture's version: campaigning changes turnout by a fixed amount, plus noise.

Turnout model

Each dot is one simulated area. Thick bars are the group averages. Noise is fixed, so moving a slider moves the same dots.

Reading the model

α
Intercept: expected turnout with no campaign. Lecture: 0.4.
β
Effect of a campaign: the gap between the two group averages. Lecture: 0.3.
campaign
0 or 1, was a campaign present.
ε
Error term: random noise centered on 0, with spread σ. Lecture: 0.1.

Systematic part: α + β·campaign, the predictable piece. Stochastic part: ε, the noise. Notice α + β·campaign is E[turnout | campaign], a conditional expectation.
